WHO Skin Disease Guidelines for Primary Healthcare Workers

SECTION 1: DERMATOPHYTOSIS (FUNGAL INFECTIONS)

Tinea corporis (Ringworm)
Clinical features: Circular or ring-shaped scaly plaques with central clearing.
Itching is common. May be single or multiple lesions.
Treatment: Topical antifungal (Clotrimazole 1%, Miconazole 2%) for 2-4 weeks.
Refer: If immunocompromised, extensive disease, or treatment failure.

Tinea pedis (Athlete's foot)
Scaling, maceration between toes. Itching and burning.
Treatment: Keep feet dry. Topical antifungal for 4 weeks.

Tinea capitis (Scalp ringworm)
Scaly patches on scalp with hair loss. Common in children.
Requires systemic antifungal (not topical alone). Refer to physician.

Pityriasis versicolor
Hypo or hyperpigmented macules on trunk. Fine scaling.
Treatment: Ketoconazole 2% shampoo or selenium sulfide applied topically.
Recurrence is common in tropical climates.

SECTION 2: SCABIES

Caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var hominis mite.
Symptoms: Intense itching, worse at night. Burrows in web spaces of fingers,
wrists, belt line, genitals. Secondary excoriation common.
Treatment: Permethrin 5% cream (first-line). Apply to whole body from neck down.
Leave overnight (8-12 hours). Repeat after 1 week.
Benzyl benzoate 25% lotion is an alternative in resource-limited settings.
All household and close contacts must be treated simultaneously.
Wash and hot-dry all clothing and bedding.

SECTION 3: ECZEMA AND DERMATITIS

Atopic Dermatitis
Chronic pruritic inflammatory condition. Associated with asthma and allergic rhinitis.
Features: Dry skin, lichenification, excoriation. Flexural involvement in older children/adults.
Management: Regular emollients (moisturizers). Avoid triggers (soap, wool, sweat).
Mild-moderate: Low-potency topical corticosteroids for flares.
Refer: Moderate-severe disease requiring potent steroids or systemic therapy.

Contact Dermatitis
Irritant contact dermatitis: From repeated contact with irritants (detergents, water).
Allergic contact dermatitis: Type IV hypersensitivity reaction.
Features: Erythema, vesicles, weeping at site of contact.
Management: Identify and avoid causative agent. Calamine for mild symptoms.
Topical corticosteroids for inflammation. Refer if severe.

SECTION 4: BACTERIAL SKIN INFECTIONS

Impetigo
Superficial bacterial infection. Honey-colored crusted lesions. Common in children.
Caused by Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes.
Treatment: Topical mupirocin for localized disease. Oral antibiotics if extensive.

Cellulitis
Deep skin infection causing spreading erythema, warmth, swelling, fever.
Requires systemic antibiotics. Refer immediately if fever or rapid spread.

Folliculitis
Infection of hair follicles. Small pustules around hair follicles.
Usually responds to topical antiseptics. Refer recurrent cases.

SECTION 5: LEPROSY (HANSEN'S DISEASE)

Caused by Mycobacterium leprae. Affects skin and peripheral nerves.
Clinical features:
- Hypopigmented or reddish patches with reduced or absent sensation
- Thickened peripheral nerves
- Weakness or numbness in hands and feet
- Skin nodules (lepromatous form)

Diagnosis: Loss of sensation in skin patch is pathognomonic.
Treatment: Multi-drug therapy (MDT) under physician supervision only.
Do not attempt treatment at primary level without specialist confirmation.
This is a notifiable disease. Report all suspected cases to district health officer.

SECTION 6: SKIN CANCER

Warning signs (ABCDE criteria):
A - Asymmetry: One half does not match the other
B - Border: Irregular, ragged, or blurred edges
C - Color: Variation in color (brown, black, red, white)
D - Diameter: Larger than 6mm (pencil eraser size)
E - Evolution: Changing in size, shape, or color

Non-healing ulcers, especially on sun-exposed skin in elderly patients.
Refer ALL suspicious lesions immediately to district hospital.
Do not biopsy or treat at primary level.

SECTION 7: HEAT-RELATED SKIN CONDITIONS

Miliaria (Heat Rash, Prickly Heat)
Caused by blocked eccrine sweat glands in hot, humid conditions.
Miliaria rubra: Red papules and vesicles. Itching and prickling sensation.
Management: Cool environment, loose cotton clothing, talc-free powder.
Avoid occlusive clothing. Usually self-limiting in cooler conditions.

SECTION 8: WHEN TO REFER

Immediate referral (same day):
- Suspected leprosy
- Suspected malignancy
- Cellulitis with systemic features
- Extensive skin loss or blistering
- Anaphylaxis

Urgent referral (within 24-48 hours):
- Moderate to severe eczema
- Infected scabies
- Impetigo in neonate or immunocompromised

Routine referral:
- Treatment failure after appropriate therapy
- Uncertain diagnosis
- Recurrent conditions requiring investigation
