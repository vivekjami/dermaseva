WHO GUIDE TO SKIN CONDITIONS IN PRIMARY CARE
Source: World Health Organization

CHAPTER 1: FUNGAL SKIN INFECTIONS

1.1 DERMATOPHYTOSIS (TINEA / RINGWORM)
Dermatophytosis encompasses infections caused by dermatophyte fungi affecting skin (tinea corporis), scalp (tinea capitis), groin (tinea cruris), feet (tinea pedis), and nails (tinea unguium/onychomycosis). Clinical features vary by site. Tinea corporis: annular lesion with central clearing, raised erythematous scaly border. Tinea cruris: erythematous patch in groin with sharp border. Diagnosis is clinical. Treatment: topical azole antifungals (clotrimazole, miconazole) for 2-4 weeks for skin infections. Scalp and nail infections require systemic treatment (griseofulvin, terbinafine) and specialist referral.

1.2 PITYRIASIS VERSICOLOR
Caused by Malassezia furfur, presents as multiple oval hypo- or hyperpigmented scaly macules on trunk, neck, upper arms. Fluoresces yellow-green under Wood's lamp. Treatment: topical ketoconazole, selenium sulfide, or zinc pyrithione shampoo. Recurrence is common.

CHAPTER 2: PARASITIC SKIN INFECTIONS

2.1 SCABIES
Human scabies is caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis. Pathognomonic sign: sinuous burrows especially in web spaces of fingers, wrists, ankles. Intense pruritus worse at night. Secondary changes include excoriations, eczematization, impetigo. Crusted (Norwegian) scabies is a hyperinfestation occurring in immunocompromised individuals with thick hyperkeratotic crusts and requires urgent specialist management. Treatment: permethrin 5% applied to all body surfaces from neck down for 8-14 hours; repeat after 1 week. Benzyl benzoate 25% is an alternative. Treat all household contacts simultaneously and wash clothing and bedding at 60°C.

CHAPTER 3: INFLAMMATORY SKIN CONDITIONS

3.1 ECZEMA (ATOPIC DERMATITIS)
Atopic dermatitis is a chronic relapsing inflammatory skin condition. Diagnostic criteria (Hanifin and Rajka): major criteria include pruritus, typical morphology and distribution, chronic relapsing course, personal or family history of atopy. Mild AD: dry skin with mild pruritus, emollients twice daily, avoid triggers. Moderate-severe AD: topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1% for face, mild-moderate potency for body) under medical supervision. Refer moderate-severe cases to PHC.

3.2 CONTACT DERMATITIS
Irritant contact dermatitis: direct skin damage by irritant substances. Allergic contact dermatitis: type IV hypersensitivity. Both present with erythema, edema, vesicles, pruritus at contact site. Management: identify and avoid trigger, cool compresses, emollients. Extensive or severe contact dermatitis requires topical corticosteroids under medical supervision.

3.3 MILIARIA (HEAT RASH / PRICKLY HEAT)
Obstruction of eccrine sweat glands. Miliaria crystallina: superficial clear vesicles, no inflammation. Miliaria rubra: erythematous papules and vesicles, pruritic. Management: cool environment, breathable clothing, avoid heavy creams and ointments.

CHAPTER 4: CONDITIONS REQUIRING URGENT REFERRAL

4.1 LEPROSY
Caused by Mycobacterium leprae. WHO classification: paucibacillary (1-5 lesions) and multibacillary (more than 5 lesions). Clinical features: hypopigmented or erythematous skin patches with definite loss of sensation; thickened, painful peripheral nerves; positive slit-skin smear for acid-fast bacilli. Early diagnosis and multidrug therapy (MDT) are essential to prevent disability. All suspected cases must be referred for MDT under the National Leprosy Eradication Programme (NLEP).

4.2 CUTANEOUS MALIGNANCIES
Basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma. Any pigmented lesion with asymmetry, irregular border, multiple colors, diameter >6mm, evolution (ABCDE criteria) must be urgently referred to dermatologist. Community health workers must not attempt to treat or reassure for these conditions.

4.3 CELLULITIS AND SKIN INFECTIONS
Spreading erythema with warmth, tenderness, systemic fever indicates bacterial skin infection (cellulitis). Requires systemic antibiotics. Refer to PHC same day. Abscess requires surgical drainage by trained personnel.

CHAPTER 5: DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS GUIDE

5.1 DIFFERENTIATING COMMON RASHES
Ringworm vs Pityriasis rosea: ringworm has annular lesion with central clearing; pityriasis rosea has herald patch followed by Christmas tree distribution.
Scabies vs insect bites: scabies has characteristic burrows in web spaces and nocturnal pruritus; insect bites are random distribution.
Eczema vs psoriasis: eczema has ill-defined borders, weeping in acute phase; psoriasis has sharply demarcated silvery plaques, typically extensor surfaces.
Contact dermatitis vs cellulitis: contact dermatitis has clear exposure history and no fever; cellulitis has systemic symptoms and spreads rapidly.
